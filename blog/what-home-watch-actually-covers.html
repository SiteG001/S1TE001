What a home watch visit actually covers

“We check on your house” is a sentence that can mean forty documented minutes or a slow drive past the mailbox. Here is the difference, written out.

Home watch is a service you are, by definition, never present for. That is an uncomfortable thing to buy. You are paying someone to look at your house and tell you what they saw, and unless you know precisely what "look" means, the only evidence you have that anything happened at all is an invoice.

So this is the route, in order, with the reasoning attached. If you are comparing providers, and you should, this doubles as a list of things to ask them about.

The route is fixed on purpose

The single most valuable property of a good inspection is that it is identical every time. Not thorough in general. Identical. Change detection is the entire mechanism. A stain on a ceiling means nothing on its own; a stain that was not there three weeks ago means a roof.

A route that varies by visit throws that away. You end up with a set of unrelated snapshots instead of a sequence, and the one thing you actually needed, did this change, becomes unanswerable.

Outside first, from the ground

The exterior gets walked before the door is opened, because the outside explains most of what the inside will show.

  • Roof and gutters from the ground. Lifted or missing shingles, debris loads, sagging or separated gutter runs, downspouts still discharging where they should. Nobody is climbing on your roof. That is a licensed roofer's job, and a home watch provider up a ladder is a liability question rather than a service.
  • Siding, trim and the full perimeter. New cracks, wood rot, insect activity, staining that indicates water tracking down a wall.
  • Grade and drainage. Where water went the last time it rained hard, visible in the mulch, the erosion channels and the foundation line.
  • Doors, windows and locks. Every one, tried. Including the ones on the back deck that nobody uses.
  • Deck, driveway, outbuildings and the treeline. Deadwood over the roof, limbs on the drive, anything that moved since last time.
  • Evidence of visitors. Tyre tracks, footprints, packages, flyers on the door, a gate left open. All of it matters at a house that is supposed to be empty.

Inside, room by room, in the same order

The interior walk is not a glance from the doorway. Every room gets entered, and the things that fail quietly get checked deliberately.

  • Ceilings and walls, corners first. Water stains announce themselves in corners and along exterior walls long before they announce themselves anywhere obvious.
  • Under every sink. Cabinet doors open, eyes and hand on the supply lines and the trap. Slow drips are invisible from standing height and expensive from the floor down.
  • Toilets, showers and tubs. Run briefly where appropriate, check for running fill valves, confirm traps have not dried out.
  • Water heater and mechanical room. The pan, the relief valve discharge, the connections, the floor around the base, any corrosion at fittings.
  • HVAC. Running as set, filter condition, condensate line clear and actually draining, no ice, no unfamiliar noises.
  • Thermostat and interior temperature. Confirmed against what the plan says it should be, not just glanced at.
  • Appliances. Refrigerator cold, ice maker not overflowing, washer hoses intact and dry.
  • Pests and moisture. Droppings, entry points, insect activity, the particular smell of a house that has gone damp.
  • Electrical panel and alarms. No tripped breakers, detectors still chirping-free and powered.
  • Basement, garage and crawl space access. Where standing water shows up first, and where most owners never go.

The report is the product

The visit is the work. The report is what you actually buy, and it should be video rather than a form.

A checklist with fifteen ticked boxes is a claim. A walk-through video is evidence: you see the rooms in sequence, you see the corners the camera lingered on, and you can go back to last month's video and compare a stain against itself. Photographs cover anything specific: a crack with a coin next to it for scale, a serial number, the state of a filter.

Two smaller things matter as much. First, that anything found is flagged with an actual severity rather than buried in a paragraph. Second, that urgent findings come as a phone call. Active water at 4 p.m. on a Friday is not an email.

What is deliberately not included

An honest scope is short. Home watch is observation and coordination. It is not trade work, and providers who blur that line are worth asking hard questions of.

  • Repairs. Licensed trades do licensed work. We meet them, watch it, and confirm it was done properly. A company that inspects its own repairs is not really inspecting.
  • Cleaning and landscaping. Coordinated and quality-checked, not performed.
  • Roof and attic entry, or anything requiring a ladder. Ground-level observation, then a roofer.
  • Security response. A home watch visit is scheduled and documented. It is not a patrol, and it is not a substitute for a monitored alarm.
  • Continuous monitoring. Between visits, sensors do the watching. A low-temperature alarm and a water sensor cost very little and cover the gap.

Five questions worth asking any provider

  1. May I see a sample report? If the answer is a PDF checklist, you now know what you are buying.
  2. Who physically visits my home, and is it the same person each time? Rotating staff cannot detect change, because they have not seen the house before.
  3. How many homes do you carry per person? There is a number past which forty documented minutes is arithmetically impossible.
  4. What happens at 2 a.m. on a holiday weekend? Listen for a specific answer, including an honest one about what is not covered.
  5. How are my access codes stored, and who else can see them?
The service is not the visit. The service is knowing, without asking, that someone walked the same route again and would have told you if anything had changed.
